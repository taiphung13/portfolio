YOURMOOD

YourMood explores how young adults can check in with their emotions, make sense of mood patterns and find a useful next step.

Product Design Mobile App

Result:

30 survey responses and five interviews informed the prototype. Six usability participants gave an average SUS score of 85/100. Their feedback led me to clarify the mood check-in and activity-creation controls.

Role
Product designer
Team
Designer + developer
Timeline
Sep 2024–Jan 2025
Research
30 survey responses, 5 interviews and 6 usability participants

Overview

Helping people move from a mood check-in to a next step

I started YourMood with a question: once someone records how they feel, what would help them decide what to do next?

I led research, product direction, interaction design, prototyping and usability testing. I worked with a developer to review feasibility and carry the design through to implementation.

Project recognition

Second Prize, Advanced and Immersive Technology — Innovation Fest 2025.

Developed as a Master’s project at Birmingham City University.

Context

Recognising a feeling is only the beginning.

I focused on adults aged 18–35 who noticed stress or changes in mood but were unsure how to respond. Before defining the product, I explored their reflection habits, what made those habits difficult to sustain and how they chose support.

Research

How people reflected on their mood

I began with an anonymous survey to understand how people noticed changes in mood, whether they reflected on them and why wellbeing apps did or did not become part of their routine.

Participants

30 adults aged 18–35 responded:

  • 17 working professionals
  • 10 university students
  • 3 postgraduate students

The sample included regular app users, people who had stopped using wellbeing apps and people who had never tried one.

Survey findings

Stress and reflection

  • 80% reported stress or noticeable mood changes several times a week or more.
  • 30% regularly recorded or reflected on their mood.
  • 33% had tried mood tracking but stopped.
  • 73% preferred a check-in under a minute. I had not yet tested completion time.

These responses pointed to a gap between noticing a feeling and making time to reflect. I took two questions into interviews: what made habits difficult to sustain, and what would make reflection feel worth the effort?

Research

Why reflection habits faded

I interviewed five people to understand what lay behind the survey responses and what they needed from support.

I asked about:

  • What prompted them to reflect
  • Why previous wellbeing habits had faded
  • How they responded to stress
  • What made support feel relevant
  • What they felt comfortable sharing

Trust, cost and choosing support mattered alongside the effort of keeping a reflection habit.

Four themes emerged:

Clear information

People wanted information they could understand and use. This reinforced the need to explain mood patterns clearly.

Choosing support

Cost and therapist suitability affected choice. Access to professional support needed its own research and design work.

Trust and privacy

People wanted to know how their information would be used. I kept extra check-in detail optional.

Flexible support

People valued flexible support. I explored self-guided activities and left therapy access for further research.

Research

What existing products offered

I reviewed Headspace, DailyBean, MoodShift and BetterHelp to compare their approaches to tracking, content and support. I looked for a useful role YourMood could play.

The opportunity

The comparison helped me focus on the connection between recording a mood and choosing what to do next:

Check in → Make sense of a pattern → Choose an activity

Synthesis

Finding patterns across the research

I grouped 86 observations and quotes into six clusters, then four themes. This helped me identify recurring needs and decide which problems to address first.

Theme 1: Frequent changes, inconsistent reflection

People often noticed stress or mood changes, but few kept a regular reflection habit.

Theme 2: Effort made habits harder to sustain

Long forms, too many choices and unclear value made wellbeing tools difficult to keep using.

Theme 3: Recording was only a starting point

People wanted to make sense of their entries, rather than simply keep a mood history.

Theme 4: Support had to fit the situation

People preferred different ways of coping depending on their mood, circumstances and available time.

Synthesis

Keeping different behaviours in view

I used two behavioural archetypes to consider how reflection habits and support needs might differ across the experience.

Synthesis

Where the journey stalled

I mapped the journey from noticing a mood change to seeking support, looking for moments when effort or uncertainty interrupted progress.

Problem to solve

Young adults need a quick, private way to reflect on their emotions and choose a useful next step.

How might we?

How might we help someone move from a brief mood check-in to a useful next step without overwhelming them?

Product direction

How research shaped the direction

I used four principles to guide feature choices:

  • Keep the first check-in short
  • Make extra context optional
  • Explain patterns without diagnosing
  • Offer activities people can choose

Product scope

What to build first

Core journey

  1. Brief mood check-in
  2. Weekly and monthly mood history
  3. Plain-language explanations of patterns
  4. Breathing, meditation and reflection activities

Supporting features

  1. Guided journalling
  2. Optional reminders
  3. Saved activities

Future opportunities

  1. Community discussions
  2. Wellbeing challenges

Structure

Information architecture

I organised the app around three goals: check in, understand patterns and choose support. Other features would help people return to that journey.

Interaction flow

From check-in to an optional activity

Home → Check in → Emotion and intensity → Optional context → Save → Review a pattern → Choose an activity

Exploration

Balancing speed with useful context

I compared a single-screen entry, a guided flow and a card-based dashboard. I chose progressive disclosure so people could record the essentials first and add context if useful. Asking for less would make entry easier, but could limit how much the app could explain.

Design decision 1

Make check-in easy to find

I had focused on making check-in brief. Testing revealed an earlier obstacle: people needed to find the entry point before they could use the flow.

What I observed

The plus in the bottom navigation did not clearly suggest mood check-in. Participants had difficulty finding it, although completing the entry was easier once they did.

What I changed

I added a clear home-screen prompt, changed the navigation icon and explored an introductory hint. These revisions made the purpose of the entry point more explicit.

Trade-off and next test

The extra entry point improves visibility but adds to the home screen. I would test the prompt, icon and introductory hint separately to understand which helps people start.

Design decision 2

Make mood patterns clearer

The research suggested that keeping a record was only part of the need. People also wanted to understand what their entries might tell them.

What informed the direction

The early dashboard showed recent entries, but a chart alone left its meaning open to interpretation. Its data was illustrative.

How I would develop it

Pair mood history with a plain-language explanation of the period and measure shown. Describe patterns carefully, without implying a diagnosis or a cause.

Trade-off and next test

A short summary can hide nuance. I would validate the calculations and test how people interpret the chart before connecting it to activity suggestions.

Design decision 3

Say what the activity button does

Activity creation exposed a similar problem. The plus icon left people to work out what the control meant, so I made its purpose explicit.

What I observed

The unlabelled plus did not consistently communicate activity creation. Participants took different routes to find it.

What I changed

I added “Create new activity” so the button explained its action. This addressed the ambiguity directly while keeping the control within the activity list.

Trade-off and next test

The label takes more space but reduces the need to interpret an icon. I would test whether people can create an activity from a task prompt that does not name the button.

Usability testing

Testing the main actions

Six participants completed seven tasks in Maze, followed by discussion over Zoom. I reviewed where they hesitated, how they interpreted labels and which actions were difficult to find.

What the study showed

The average SUS score was 85/100 across six participants. Task observations also revealed unclear controls, which informed decisions 1 and 3. A follow-up test is needed to assess the revisions.

SUS reflects how usable the prototype felt. Some task prompts named interface controls, which may have helped discovery. I would use neutral, goal-based prompts in the next round.

Delivery and reflection

What I would do next

I worked with a developer on components, interaction states and implementation. The project taught me to test essential actions before expanding scope. Next, I would focus on check-in, reflection and one activity, then test whether people can find value and return to the journey.
